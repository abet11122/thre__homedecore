Choose one scene, put it where it is seen from the pavement, give it an edge and a light source, and leave the rest of the garden dark. A single concentrated idea beats props spread thinly across a lawn every time.

The common failure is distribution. Twelve items placed evenly over a garden read as objects on grass. The same twelve grouped into one corner, lit from one side, read as a scene. Nothing is bought or built differently — only the arrangement changes.

## Treat darkness as a material

This is the idea that matters most, and it costs nothing.

An unlit garden with one lit area is far more effective than an evenly lit garden. The eye goes to the bright thing and fills in the rest, which means a small well-lit scene can carry an entire frontage.

**Light one zone.** Everything outside it stays dark on purpose.

**Light from low and to one side.** A light at ground level throwing upwards gives long shadows and a shape that reads from the road. A light shining straight at something flattens it and reveals exactly how thin the material is.

**Avoid lighting the whole lawn.** Floodlighting a garden removes the only advantage the season gives you.

## Pick a zone and give it an edge

A scene needs a boundary, or it reads as things that have drifted.

**Against the house wall.** The easiest option, because the wall is a backdrop and power is near. Good for anything flat or propped.

**A corner with planting behind it.** A hedge or shrubs give depth and hide the edges. The best option if you have one.

**Beside the path, set back.** Close enough to read, far enough that nobody walks into it. Keep the path itself completely clear.

**The centre of an open lawn.** The hardest to make work, because there is no backdrop and the scene has to succeed from every angle.

Whatever you choose, mark the edge with something physical — a low fence, a line of stones, a border of straw. The edge is what converts a group of objects into a deliberate display, and it also stops people walking in.

## Build in three depths

A scene with everything at one distance looks flat. Three depths give it body.

1. **Background** — the tallest layer. Bare branches staked upright, a sheet of dark fabric against a fence, a tall shape against the wall.
2. **Middle** — the main subject at roughly waist to chest height. This is where the detail goes, because it is where the eye lands.
3. **Foreground** — low things at ground level: straw, fallen branches, low lights, a pumpkin or two. These give scale and hide the bases of everything behind.

Leave gaps between the layers. The shadows that fall into those gaps do as much as the props.

## What actually survives late October

Weather decides how long the display lasts, and most disappointment is material choice.

**Stake or weight everything, without exception.** Tent pegs and guy lines for anything tall. Gravel, sand or paving slabs in the base of pots. A hollow prop with nothing in it will be down the road after the first gusty night.

**Assume paper and card will fail** unless they are under cover. Hessian, wood, plastic sheet, painted board and natural material all cope with rain.

**Expect the ground to be soft.** Anything heavy on a lawn sinks and leaves marks that last into spring. A board underneath spreads the load.

**Take it down promptly.** Material left out past the first week of November deteriorates fast and starts to look abandoned rather than seasonal.

## Power outdoors, which is the part to get right

Garden runs are longer than porch runs, which makes the electrical side more important rather than less.

- Outdoor-rated leads and outdoor-rated lights only, IP44 or better.
- Plug into an **RCD-protected** socket, or use a plug-in RCD adaptor.
- Bury cable under a plank or tape it down along an edge. Never leave it across a path or hidden in grass where a mower or a foot will find it.
- Keep every connection raised off wet ground, inside a weatherproof box if it will sit out for weeks.
- Total the wattage before chaining several things to one lead.

Battery-powered lights avoid all of this and are the sensible choice for anything placed away from the house. Cold shortens battery life noticeably, so expect fewer hours than the packaging claims in late October.

## Fog, and why it usually disappoints

Fog machines look remarkable in photographs taken indoors and often do very little in a garden.

Fog disperses in any wind at all, so it needs a still night and an enclosed corner — against a wall, inside a porch, between hedges. In the open it vanishes in seconds. Low-lying fog needs chilling to stay at ground level; an unchilled machine sends it straight up and away.

If the forecast shows any breeze, spend the money on lighting instead. Lighting works every night; fog works on perhaps one in four.

## Sizing it to the garden you have

**A small front garden or a few square metres.** One scene against the wall, one light, three pumpkins. Resist spreading out — a small garden gains more from concentration than a large one does.

**A medium garden with a path.** One scene beside the path plus a lit approach. Keep everything on one side so the walking route stays obviously clear, which is the same logic as [reading a porch from the street inwards](/post/halloween-porch-decor-ideas/).

**A large garden.** Two scenes at most, well separated, each with its own light. Two good scenes with dark space between them beat a continuous band of props, and the dark space is what makes each one land.

## Keeping it usable for the people who visit

A garden on Halloween night has children walking through it in poor light, often in costumes that restrict how well they can see.

Keep the route to the door lit and clear. Leave nothing protruding at ankle or face height along it. Place anything intended to startle well away from the path, where somebody stepping back cannot fall off a step. If the scene is genuinely frightening, a light on the gate gives people the choice to approach or not — which is the courteous version, and the one that gets a better reaction anyway.
