Decide which side you are decorating for before anything goes up. A window faces two audiences, and what reads well from the pavement often looks like the back of something from the armchair.

This is the one surface in the house with that problem, and it is why window decorating so often disappoints. The arrangement is built from inside, standing at the glass, which is neither of the two views that matter.

## Pick the audience

**A front window onto a street or a path** is mostly for other people. Build it to read from outside: bold shapes, a clear silhouette, light behind rather than in front. Accept that from the sofa you will be looking at the reverse of it.

**A window at the back, or a flat high up,** is yours. Build it for the room — detail at close range, texture, things that catch a lamp.

**A window in a room nobody sits in** is the easiest of all, because only one view exists.

Trying to satisfy both at once produces something that half-works twice. Choose.

## Light from behind, not at the glass

This is the single most useful thing on this page, and it costs nothing.

A lamp placed behind an object throws its outline outward, which is what makes a shape read from the street after dark. A light shining at the window reflects off the glass and shows the room instead, along with everyone in it.

**Keep the room lights low.** The commonest reason window lights look weak from outside is that a bright ceiling light behind them is winning. Turn the overhead off and a modest string of lights reads clearly from the pavement.

**Warm white, and nothing flashing.** A window sits in your field of view all evening from inside the room. A flashing light there is tiring within twenty minutes, and it is no better for the neighbours.

**Put the light low.** A lamp at sill height throwing upward gives longer shadows and a better outline than one at head height.

## Hanging things without marking anything

Three fixings cover almost every window, and none of them touch painted frames.

**A tension rod inside the reveal.** The best option by a distance. It holds a garland, a row of hanging shapes or a light string, it adjusts to the width, and it leaves nothing behind. Measure the reveal depth as well as the width — a rod needs a few centimetres of depth to brace against.

**Suction hooks on the glass.** Good for a wreath or a single hanging shape. Clean the pane with rubbing alcohol first, press firmly, and expect to re-seat it once as the temperature swings.

**Removable adhesive on the glass, not the frame.** Glass is the most forgiving surface for adhesive and painted timber is among the worst. If you must use the frame, follow the removal technique set out in [the renter's door guide](/post/apartment-christmas-door-decor-ideas/) and pull the tab straight down, never outward.

## Condensation, which decides what survives

Any cold pane in a warm room produces water, and single glazing produces a great deal of it.

Paper stuck to the glass soaks it up, cockles within a week, and can lift paint when it comes away. Card silhouettes, paper snowflakes and anything printed should sit a few centimetres clear of the pane — hung from a rod rather than taped to the glass.

Greenery against cold glass stays fresher than greenery over a radiator, which is one of the few places the cold works in your favour. Keep the battery pack of any light string off the sill itself, where water collects.

## Three arrangements that work

**A wreath on the glass.** One hook, one wreath, hung so it sits in the middle of the pane rather than at the top. From outside it reads immediately; from inside it is a silhouette against the daylight.

**A row of hanging shapes from a rod.** Three, five or seven at staggered drops, never a level row. Cut shapes, dried orange slices, small baubles on ribbon. This is the arrangement that looks equally deliberate from both sides, which makes it the safe choice when you cannot decide.

**A lit sill.** A line of small lanterns, candle-shaped lights or short jars along the sill, with the room light off. The simplest of the three and the strongest from the street. Keep it clear of long curtains.

## When the window is the main event

In a flat with no porch and no front door of your own, the window does the work the doorstep would otherwise do, and it is seen by far more people than a shared corridor is.

That makes it worth more effort than it usually gets. The three-surface limit that keeps a small flat from looking cluttered is set out in [the renter's seasonal guide](/post/small-apartment-halloween-decor-ideas/), and a window is almost always worth being one of the three. The greenery it gets dressed with is the same material as [the table arrangement](/post/christmas-table-centerpieces/), cut from the same trimmings, and the sill below it follows the same restraint as [the entry table](/post/entryway-table-decor-ideas/) — a landing zone first, decoration second.
