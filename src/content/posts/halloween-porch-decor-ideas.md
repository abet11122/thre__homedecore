A porch is looked at from three distances, and each one needs a different thing. From the pavement, only silhouette and light register. From the path, texture and detail start to appear. At the doorstep, the only thing that matters is that people can get to the door safely.

Most porches are decorated at arm's length, standing on the step, which is the one distance almost nobody sees them from. Walk to the pavement first and look back. That view is the one doing the work on the night.

## Distance one: the pavement, about 10 metres out

At this range the eye resolves shape and brightness and nothing else. Small painted details, clever labels and delicate arrangements are entirely invisible.

**Work in silhouette.** One tall shape against a lit background does more than twenty scattered objects. A bare branch in a weighted pot, a tall cut-out shape propped beside the door, a cluster of three pumpkins of genuinely different sizes.

**Light from behind, not from the front.** A light placed behind an object throws its outline outward, which is what makes a shape read at distance. Front lighting flattens everything and shows the plastic.

**Pick one colour of light and hold to it.** Amber reads warm and traditional, cold white reads clinical, green reads unsettling, and all three together read as a muddle. A single colour across the whole frontage is what makes a modest display look deliberate.

**Group in odd numbers and vary the heights properly.** Three pumpkins at 20cm, 35cm and 50cm make a group. Three at 28cm, 30cm and 32cm make a row that looks like an error.

## Distance two: the path, two or three metres out

Now detail begins to pay off, and this is where the arrangement should reward a second look.

- **Texture against texture.** Dried corn stalks tied to a post, hessian, a rough blanket over a chair, straw at the base of a planter. Natural texture photographs well and survives rain better than paper.
- **Layer heights along the approach.** Something at ground level, something at about knee height, something at head height. Eyes travel up as people walk in.
- **Repeat one motif three times** along the path rather than using three different ideas once each. Repetition reads as a scheme; variety reads as whatever was left in the box.
- **Use the handrail or the posts.** Vertical surfaces are usually ignored and they are at exactly eye level on the approach.

> If you only do one thing on the path, line it. Low lights or small lanterns down one edge give an arrival sequence, and they solve the safety problem at the same time.

## Distance three: the doorstep, where restraint matters

This is where enthusiasm causes actual problems, so the rules here are practical rather than decorative.

**Keep 90cm of clear walking width.** That is enough for an adult holding a child's hand, which is the traffic you will actually get. Measure it rather than estimating.

**Leave the edge of every step visible.** Do not run anything across a tread, and do not place a mat, a sheet or trailing fabric where a foot lands. A step edge obscured by decoration in the dark is the single most likely thing to hurt somebody.

**Nothing at ankle height in the walking line.** Cables, low props and wire frames belong at the edges, never crossing the route to the door.

**Keep the door itself light.** A wreath and one hanging element is enough. Anything bulky gets knocked every time the door opens, and the door will open a great deal that evening.

## Pumpkins that last more than four days

Carved pumpkins collapse fast, and the main cause is warmth and moisture rather than time.

- Carve as late as you reasonably can — two days before is better than a week.
- Scrape the inner wall down to about 2.5cm thickness so it dries evenly rather than rotting in patches.
- Keep them out of direct sun and off warm stone during the day.
- Use battery tea lights inside. A real flame cooks the flesh from within and accelerates the collapse, quite apart from the fire risk among dry material.
- Uncarved pumpkins last weeks. A group of whole pumpkins with one carved among them is the low-effort version that still looks intentional.

## Electricity outdoors, done properly

This is the part worth taking seriously, because a wet extension lead is a real hazard rather than a theoretical one.

- Use an **outdoor-rated** extension lead and outdoor-rated lights, IP44 as a minimum.
- Plug into an **RCD-protected** socket. If you are unsure whether yours is, a plug-in RCD adaptor is inexpensive.
- Run cable along the edge of the porch, taped down, never across the walking route.
- Keep every connection off the ground and out of standing water — a connection sitting in a puddle is the usual failure.
- Add up the wattage before you plug a chain of things into one lead.

Solar is the low-risk alternative, with one caveat worth knowing: the panel needs several hours of real direct sun to charge, and late October rarely provides it. Treat solar as a supplement rather than the main source.

## Weather, which decides what survives

Late October brings wind and rain, and most disappointment comes from materials that cannot take either.

**Weight everything.** Gravel or sand in the base of light pots and props. Anything tall and hollow will be in the road by morning otherwise.

**Assume paper will fail.** Paper, card and fabric need a sheltered position under a roof or a porch overhang. In the open they last until the first proper shower.

**Check it the morning after a windy night** rather than discovering a prop in a neighbour's garden.

## Scaling it to the porch you actually have

**A single step with no cover.** Two pumpkins beside the door, one lit shape, a wreath. Everything weather-resistant, nothing paper. Done in twenty minutes.

**A covered porch.** The overhang changes what is possible — hanging elements, fabric, paper and lanterns all survive. Use the ceiling, since it is the surface everyone forgets.

**A deep porch with room to stand.** Build a small scene: a chair, a blanket, a lantern and a table.
