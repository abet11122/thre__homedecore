Eight projects below, each with what it needs, roughly how long it takes, and the step where it usually goes wrong. Times are estimates for a first attempt. Six take an evening; two need a weekend because something has to dry.

No prices are given here. Material costs vary too much by country, shop and year for a figure to mean anything, and a made-up number is worse than none.

## 1. Dried orange and bay garland

**Needs:** oranges, a sharp knife, a baking tray, baking paper, string, a needle, bay leaves.
**Time:** 20 minutes active, 3 to 4 hours in the oven.
**Where it goes wrong:** slices cut too thick never dry properly and go mouldy within a week.

Slice oranges to about 4mm — thinner than feels right. Blot both sides hard with kitchen paper, lay them on baking paper without overlapping, and put them in the oven at its lowest setting, around 80 to 100°C, with the door propped slightly ajar so moisture escapes. Turn them every hour.

They are done when the flesh is translucent and leathery rather than tacky. Thread with a needle, alternating with bay leaves. It holds its scent for weeks and lives in a paper bag between years.

## 2. Black wax dipped candles

**Needs:** plain pillar or taper candles, black wax or wax crayons, a heatproof jug, a deep pan.
**Time:** about 45 minutes including cooling.
**Where it goes wrong:** wax too hot strips the candle instead of coating it.

Melt the wax in a jug standing in a pan of simmering water — never over direct heat. Let it cool slightly until a skin just begins to form at the edge, then dip the candle in one smooth movement and lift it straight out.

Hang it to set for a few minutes and dip again for a deeper finish. Two or three dips give an uneven, slightly drip-marked surface, which is the point. Stand them in heavy holders well clear of anything dry.

## 3. Ink-dipped pale pumpkins

**Needs:** pale pumpkins, black emulsion or acrylic, a wide container, gloves.
**Time:** 15 minutes active, overnight to dry.
**Where it goes wrong:** dipping too far up. The effect depends on the pale part staying dominant.

Pour paint into a container to a depth of 3 to 4cm. Hold the pumpkin by the stalk and lower the base in, then lift it out slowly so the paint runs back and leaves a soft edge rather than a hard line.

Stand it on baking paper to dry overnight. Dip no more than a third of the pumpkin's height. These last several weeks because nothing is cut, which is their advantage over a carved one.

## 4. Branch and ribbon door spray

**Needs:** bare branches, dark ribbon, florist's wire or garden twine, secateurs.
**Time:** 30 minutes.
**Where it goes wrong:** making it symmetrical, which turns a spray into a bad wreath.

Cut five to seven branches of clearly different lengths — the longest roughly twice the shortest. Hold them as a loose fan, bind the stems tightly about a third of the way up, and tie a long ribbon over the binding.

Hang it from a single hook. A spray takes a fraction of the time a wreath does, stores flat against a wall, and reads well on a door that gets knocked constantly. On a rented door, hang it from a removable hook and read the removal technique in [the renter's guide](/post/small-apartment-halloween-decor-ideas/) before sticking anything to paint.

## 5. Jar lanterns with cut paper

**Needs:** clean glass jars, black paper, a scalpel or small scissors, a cutting mat, battery tea lights.
**Time:** about an hour for three.
**Where it goes wrong:** cutting detail too fine, which disappears entirely once lit.

Cut simple bold shapes — a bare tree, a cat in outline, a moon, a plain arch. Anything thinner than about 3mm closes up visually when the light is behind it.

Wrap the paper inside the jar rather than outside, where it stays dry and the glass protects it. Use battery tea lights, since a real flame inside a jar packed with paper is a poor idea. Three jars of different heights grouped together read far better than one on its own.

## 6. Hessian and dried grass wreath

**Needs:** a wire or willow wreath base, hessian strips, dried grasses or wheat, florist's wire.
**Time:** 1 to 2 hours.
**Where it goes wrong:** spreading material evenly all the way round, which looks machine-made.

Wrap the base in overlapping hessian strips and wire the ends out of sight. Then build the dried material in one direction only, covering roughly two-thirds of the ring and leaving the remaining third as bare wrapped base.

The asymmetry is what makes it look made rather than bought. Wire each small bundle facing the same way, overlapping the previous bundle's stems so none are visible. It stores flat in a bag and will keep for years if it stays dry.

## 7. Smoke-blackened glass votives

**Needs:** plain glass votives, a candle, a heatproof surface, clear matte spray varnish.
**Time:** 20 minutes active, an hour to cure.
**Where it goes wrong:** skipping the varnish, after which the soot comes off on everybody's hands.

Hold a glass votive above a candle flame, moving it continuously, so soot deposits unevenly up the sides. Build it gradually, keeping the glass moving to avoid heat-stressing one spot. Let it cool completely.

Then seal with a light coat of clear matte varnish from a distance of about 30cm, or every surface it touches will be marked. Work somewhere ventilated. The result is a smoky graduated glass that looks considerably more expensive than it is.

## 8. Dark dried arrangement in a weighted jug

**Needs:** a heavy jug or vase, bare branches, dried grasses, dark seed heads, floral foam or sand.
**Time:** 30 minutes, and it lasts all season.
**Where it goes wrong:** too many stems, which closes up the gaps the arrangement depends on.

Weight the base with sand or gravel. Start with three branches at clearly different heights, pushed in at slight angles rather than straight up. Add grasses to fill the middle, then two or three dark seed heads as the visual full stop.

Leave gaps. A dried arrangement works through silhouette, which means the spaces between stems do as much as the stems. This is the one project here that carries straight through to the rest of autumn and into winter, and it follows the same three-height logic as [a styled mantel](/post/autumn-mantel-styling/).

## Working to one palette

The eight projects share a deliberately narrow range: black, rust, bone, bay green, aged brass. That is why they sit together rather than looking like eight separate craft afternoons.

If you make only three, make the garland, the jar lanterns and the dried arrangement. Between them they cover scent, light and height, which is most of what a room needs for the season. Scaled up for a table, the same restraint is set out in [the centrepiece guide](/post/halloween-centerpieces/); scaled outdoors, [the porch guide](/post/halloween-porch-decor-ideas/) covers what survives rain.

## Storing what you have made

Everything here packs down, which is the reason these eight were chosen over props and large builds.

Dried oranges and bay go in a paper bag, never plastic, which traps moisture and finishes them. The wreath and the door spray hang flat. Cut paper goes between two sheets of stiff card. Candles and votives need only to be kept out of the heat and away from direct sun, which fades black wax to a dull grey over a summer in a loft.
