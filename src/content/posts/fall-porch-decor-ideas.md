Decorate the porch in three stages rather than all at once. Foliage and chrysanthemums in early September, pumpkins in the first week of October, evergreen and lanterns in November. Nothing then has to be thrown away halfway through, and the entrance looks right for three months instead of three weeks.

The usual approach — one big arrangement built in early September — peaks too soon. Everything organic in it is past its best by the time anyone is actually looking at porches, and by mid-November it is visibly dying.

## Stage one: early September, the structure

What goes out now has to last through all three stages, so choose for durability rather than for seasonal signalling.

**Two large pots, not five small ones.** This is the most useful rule here. From the street, one substantial planter reads clearly and five small pots read as clutter. Large pots also hold moisture, so they need watering every few days rather than daily.

**Chrysanthemums, bought in bud.** Buy them with the buds still mostly closed and they will give six weeks rather than two. Open flowers at the garden centre mean the clock is already well advanced. They want watering at the base, not over the flowers, which rot if they stay wet.

**A foliage wreath that is not autumn-specific.** Eucalyptus, bay, olive or mixed dried grasses carry through to November. A wreath covered in orange leaves commits you to one stage only.

**Something with height.** Corn stalks tied to a post, a tall grass in a pot, or bare branches. Height is what stops a porch reading as a row of objects at ground level.

## Stage two: the first week of October, the pumpkins

This is the timing that matters most, and almost everyone gets it wrong by a month.

A pumpkin bought in early September is collapsing by the last week of October. Bought in the first week of October, uncarved and kept off warm stone, it will comfortably see out the season and often last into December.

**Uncarved lasts weeks; carved lasts days.** A group of whole pumpkins with one carved among them, cut two days before you actually want it, is the version that stays looking well.

**Vary size properly.** Three pumpkins at 20cm, 35cm and 50cm make a group. Three of nearly the same size make a row. Odd numbers, clustered rather than spaced evenly along the step.

**Raise them off the ground.** A pumpkin sitting directly on damp stone or wood rots from the base first and marks the surface underneath. A board, a tray, an upturned crate or a stack of two gives air underneath and adds height at the same time.

**Pale over orange, if you have the choice.** White, grey-green and buff pumpkins look considerably better against most brick and render, and they do not fight a painted door.

## Stage three: November, swapping towards winter

Rather than clearing everything on the first of November, replace what has gone over.

The chrysanthemums come out. Evergreen offcuts, pine, and anything with berries go into the same pots. The pumpkins stay as long as they are sound — a firm pumpkin in November is a perfectly good winter object and nobody reads it as a leftover.

Lanterns matter more now, because it is dark by late afternoon. This is the stage that leads naturally into [outdoor Christmas decorating](/post/outdoor-christmas-decor-ideas/) without a bare fortnight in between.

## Keeping the entrance usable

A porch is a route to a door before it is a display, and this is where enthusiasm causes real problems.

**90cm of clear walking width,** measured rather than guessed. That is enough for an adult carrying shopping or holding a child's hand.

**Every step edge visible.** Nothing across a tread, no trailing fabric where a foot lands, no mat that shifts. A step edge hidden by decoration in poor light is the most likely thing here to hurt somebody.

**Nothing at ankle height in the walking line.** Pots, cables and low props belong at the edges.

**Think about the door swing.** Anything within the arc of the door gets knocked daily and will be damaged by October.

## What survives the weather

Late autumn brings wind and persistent rain, and most disappointment comes down to material choice.

**Weight everything.** Gravel or sand in the base of light pots. Anything tall and hollow goes over in the first proper gale.

**Assume paper and card will fail** unless they sit under a roof. Hessian, wood, metal, ceramic and natural dried material all cope.

**Watch for animals.** Squirrels and foxes will take an interest in pumpkins, particularly carved ones. There is no reliable prevention; raising them helps a little, and buying late helps more.

**Check fixings after a windy night** rather than finding a wreath in the road.

## Scaling it to what you have

**A single step, no cover.** Two pots either side of the door, one wreath, three pumpkins on the step. Everything weather-resistant. Twenty minutes.

**A covered porch.** The roof changes what is possible — hanging lanterns, fabric, dried material and paper all survive. Use the ceiling, which is the surface everyone forgets.

**A deep porch with room to stand.** A chair, a blanket over its arm, a lantern and a small table beside it. Keep the walking route to one side rather than running it through the middle of the arrangement.
