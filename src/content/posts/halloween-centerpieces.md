Decide what the evening is before you buy anything. A seated dinner needs a low arrangement under about 30cm so people can see each other. A family supper needs something that can be shoved aside for serving dishes. A party table needs height, because nobody is sitting down.

Most centrepiece disappointment comes from building the wrong one — a tall dramatic arrangement on a table where six people then spend three hours talking around it.

## The seated dinner: low, long, and out of the sightline

The constraint here is conversation. Anything taller than roughly 30cm blocks the person opposite, and it will get moved during the meal whatever it looks like.

**Run it lengthways rather than massing it in the middle.** A low line down the centre of the table reads as generous and leaves the space either side usable.

**Build on a runner or a board.** Not just for looks — it means the whole thing lifts away in one movement when someone brings food to the table.

**What to use:** small pumpkins in a single colour, dark trailing foliage, a line of short candles in heavy holders, a few branches laid flat rather than standing. Dark red and near-black flowers read far better by candlelight than orange does.

**Leave two clear zones** at either end for the dishes people actually put down. A centrepiece occupying the entire table surface is one that ends the evening on the floor.

> Sit down at the table before you finish. The seated eyeline is the only view that matters and it is not the view you get while arranging.

## The family supper: one object, moved easily

For a table that gets used every day through the last week of October, the right answer is one thing rather than an arrangement.

A wide shallow bowl holding seasonal material is the most practical option there is. It changes with a handful of new contents, it lifts off with one hand, and it does not look wrong if it ends up on the side table for two days. The same bowl carries the rest of the year as well, which is the whole argument in [the dough bowl guide](/post/dough-bowl-centrepiece-ideas/).

Fill it with small pumpkins and gourds, or walnuts, or dried seed heads with two or three short candles pushed down among them. Nothing precious, nothing that minds being knocked.

## The party table: height, and nobody sitting down

A standing party inverts every rule above. Now height is the point, because the table is seen over the heads of people standing around it.

- **Go tall and narrow.** A branch arrangement in a weighted vase, a tiered stand, candlesticks of genuinely different heights.
- **Weight the base properly.** A tall arrangement on a table people reach across will go over at some point in the evening unless the base is heavy.
- **Keep the footprint small.** Table surface is needed for glasses and food. Height costs nothing in floor area; width costs everything.
- **Light it from below if you can.** A light source under or within the arrangement throws shadow upwards and does more after dark than any amount of detail.

## Candles, and the honest safety note

Candlelight is most of the effect, and a Halloween table is also the worst possible place for an open flame — dried material, paper, trailing foliage and sleeves reaching across.

Use battery tea lights inside anything enclosed: carved pumpkins, lanterns, jars, anything with material packed around it. The flicker is good enough at that scale and nobody will inspect it.

Keep real flame to tapers in heavy holders, standing clear of anything dry, in a position where nobody reaches over them. Never leave them burning in an empty room. The same reasoning applies to [an autumn mantel](/post/autumn-mantel-styling/), where dried wheat and a real candle are a poor combination.

## Colour, which decides whether it looks cheap

Shop Halloween is orange and purple plastic. The restrained version uses a much narrower range and reads far better in low light.

- **Near-black** — dark foliage, black candles, deep plum flowers
- **Rust and oxblood** — the useful warm notes, which candlelight flatters
- **Bone and parchment** — pale pumpkins, dried grasses, unbleached wax
- **One metal** — brass or aged gold, not chrome

Pale pumpkins photograph better by candlelight than orange ones, which read muddy brown once the main light is off. A single orange element in an otherwise dark scheme does more than a table full of it.

## Building it to come apart

Whatever the evening, assemble on something that lifts: a tray, a board, a runner, a length of hessian.

A centrepiece that has to be dismantled object by object to serve food is one that gets dismantled badly, usually by a guest trying to help. Built on a tray, it comes off in one movement, goes back in one movement, and survives the meal intact.
